## 1. BLOG POST

**Title:** The blood marker that predicts your heart risk better than cholesterol gets all the attention

Most people have had a cholesterol test. Almost no one has had their omega-3 index measured. That gap matters, because the omega-3 index, the percentage of EPA and DHA in the membranes of red blood cells, is increasingly recognised as one of the more informative cardiovascular and longevity markers available. Unlike cholesterol, which can shift week to week, the omega-3 index reflects the last 120 days of your status. It is a long-term signal, not a snapshot of last night's dinner.

The figures from the research are striking. Studies comparing people with a high omega-3 index of around 8% to those with a low index of around 4% have found roughly 90% lower risk of sudden cardiac death in the high group. A separate analysis found that people with a low omega-3 index carried a mortality risk comparable to smokers with a high omega-3 index, meaning low omega-3 status appeared to carry a similar signal to smoking in those studies. Across the evidence base, a high versus low index is associated with approximately five additional years of life expectancy. US and UK averages sit near the low end. Japan, where average omega-3 intake and life expectancy are both notably higher, averages around 10%.

Part of the reason for this is mechanistic. EPA and DHA do not simply dampen inflammation the way many anti-inflammatory compounds do. They are precursors to a class of signalling molecules called resolvins and protectins that actively resolve inflammatory processes once they have served their purpose. Without sufficient omega-3, that resolution step is impaired. They also maintain the fluidity of cell and vascular membranes, which affects how efficiently cells communicate and how blood moves through vessels. Two large randomised controlled trials, REDUCE-IT and VITAL, found meaningful reductions in cardiovascular events in people supplementing with omega-3. Separately, around 1.1 g per day of combined EPA and DHA appears to be a threshold above which epigenetic ageing shows slowing in the research.

Getting from a low index to a high one is practical. Most people will move from the low to the optimal range with around 1.6 to 2 g per day of combined EPA and DHA, achievable through oily fish three times per week or a quality concentrated supplement. The more important step is measuring first, because starting points vary considerably. A recheck at three to four months gives confirmation that the intervention is working.

The omega-3 index is not a standard component of NHS tests or most private health checks. It should be. If you are making decisions about cardiovascular health without knowing this number, you are working with an incomplete picture.
