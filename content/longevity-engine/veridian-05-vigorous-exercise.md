## 1. BLOG POST

**Title:** Four minutes a day: the most powerful longevity "drug" is also the cheapest

Not all exercise is equal, and the word vigorous has a specific meaning in this context. It does not mean an hour at the gym. It means an intensity at which you cannot hold a full conversation: where you can manage a few words at most before needing to breathe. That threshold, maintained for a cumulative total of around four minutes per day, is associated with approximately 36% lower all-cause mortality and around 30% lower cardiovascular mortality in large population studies. That is not four minutes of warmup. That is four minutes at a level that actually makes conversation difficult.

The leverage of vigorous effort compared to lower intensities is one of the more striking things in this area of research. One vigorous minute is associated with roughly the same all-cause mortality benefit as approximately four minutes of moderate-intensity effort. For heart attack and stroke specifically, the ratio is approximately one vigorous minute to eight minutes of moderate exercise. For cancer risk, one vigorous minute appears comparable to around two and a half hours of gentle walking. These are population-level associations from large datasets, not controlled laboratory conditions, but they are consistent across multiple analyses and the signal is strong enough to be clinically meaningful.

This has practical implications for people who do not have time or access for structured exercise. VILPA, vigorous intermittent lifestyle physical activity, describes the incidental vigorous effort that accumulates outside a gym context: taking stairs fast enough to feel the effort, walking to a meeting at a pace that raises your heart rate substantially, carrying bags uphill. Research has found that VILPA carries a comparable mortality benefit to structured gym workouts. The body responds to the physiological stimulus, not the equipment or the setting.

The structural benefits extend beyond mortality statistics. A study from Levine and colleagues found that previously sedentary 50-year-olds who undertook a two-year structured aerobic training programme had hearts that were structurally similar to those of people approximately 20 years younger. The heart retains significant plasticity in midlife given sufficient training stimulus. On the cognitive side, vigorous exercise raises blood lactate, which signals the brain to produce BDNF, a growth factor associated with memory, learning, and the formation of new neurons. The cognitive sharpening following a vigorous bout is near-immediate and documented across multiple research settings.

Cardiorespiratory fitness is one of the strongest predictors of longevity available, and low fitness behaves in the data in a way that resembles a disease in terms of its contribution to early mortality. The most important insight from the evidence is not that exercise is good. Most people already know that. It is that vigorous effort specifically, even in very small quantities distributed across a day, appears to carry a disproportionate return relative to the time it requires.

*This is general health information, not personal medical advice. If you have a heart condition or have not exercised regularly for some time, please check with your clinician before undertaking vigorous physical activity.
