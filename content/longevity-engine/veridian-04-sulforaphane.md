## 1. BLOG POST

**Title:** The compound in broccoli sprouts that helps your body clean up after city living

There is a compound found in cruciferous vegetables, broccoli, kale, cabbage, cauliflower, that does something unusual. It does not act as an antioxidant in the conventional sense. Instead, it activates a genetic switch called NRF2, a master regulator of the body's own antioxidant and detoxification systems. When NRF2 is switched on, cells upregulate production of glutathione, the body's primary internally produced antioxidant, and increase expression of phase-2 detoxification enzymes that help clear harmful compounds. Rather than supplying an external antioxidant, sulforaphane prompts the body to upregulate its own defences. That is a meaningfully different mechanism, and it is one that appears to matter in human trials.

The human evidence is specific. In a clinical study, eating watercress daily for eight weeks reduced markers of oxidative DNA damage by 17 to 24%. Smokers eating broccoli for ten days reduced DNA lesions by approximately 40%. In a study conducted in a heavily polluted region of China, participants drinking broccoli sprout extract increased their excretion of benzene, a known carcinogen, by approximately 61% within 24 hours. Early-phase clinical work in low-risk prostate cancer has found associations with slowed PSA rise in some patients. DNA damage is an upstream driver of both cancer and biological ageing, so reducing it reliably across multiple studies carries weight even where individual trial sizes are modest.

The catch is the source. Broccoli sprouts contain roughly 100 times more of the sulforaphane precursor, glucoraphanin, than mature broccoli. The compound only forms when the plant is chewed or torn and the precursor comes into contact with an enzyme called myrosinase. Cooking destroys that enzyme. So standard cooked broccoli, the usual portion, delivers relatively little. If you want meaningful doses from food, sprouts are the most practical route. Alternatively, adding a small amount of mustard seed powder to cooked broccoli restores approximately four times the bioavailability by supplying external myrosinase. A stable supplement standardised for sulforaphane content is a practical alternative for those who find food sources inconvenient.

The context for why this matters extends beyond the laboratory. City living brings chronic low-level exposure to air pollutants, traffic fumes, and other environmental compounds that produce oxidative stress and contribute to DNA damage over time. The body's NRF2-driven detoxification capacity is one of the primary defence systems against this kind of background burden. The evidence suggests sulforaphane meaningfully supports that capacity, and the benzene excretion data in particular is a direct demonstration of increased real-world clearance.

This is not a dramatic intervention. It is a consistent, low-cost source of support for systems that matter for long-term health. Like most of what actually moves the needle in longevity medicine, the benefit is slow, quiet, and compounds over time.
