## 1. BLOG POST

**Title:** Vitamin D isn't really a vitamin — and that changes everything

The name is an accident of history. When researchers first identified it in the early twentieth century, they assumed it was a dietary nutrient like the others in the series. It isn't. Vitamin D3, once absorbed from sunlight or a supplement, converts in your liver and kidneys into a steroid hormone. That hormone enters the nucleus of cells and switches genes on or off. Estimates suggest it regulates somewhere around 1,000 genes, roughly 5% of the entire human genome. That is not what most people picture when they reach for a supermarket bottle in October.

This matters because around 70% of UK adults are not getting enough of it, and not because of carelessness. The problem is structural. UK winters do not produce sufficient UVB radiation for any meaningful skin synthesis between roughly October and April. Pile on sunscreen, indoor working patterns, the fact that a 70-year-old makes approximately four times less vitamin D from the same sun exposure as a 20-year-old, higher melanin, and excess body fat (which sequesters vitamin D away from the bloodstream), and you have a population where low vitamin D status is the statistical norm, not the exception.

The downstream associations are not trivial. Research using Mendelian randomisation, a study design that uses genetic variants linked to lower vitamin D as a proxy for lifelong low status, has found links to higher all-cause mortality and roughly 50% higher dementia risk in those with genetically lower levels. Separately, in people who were deficient, correcting that status over time was associated with measurable slowing of epigenetic, biological ageing, with one larger trial finding an approximate 2.6-year slowing among deficient participants. These are associations from research, not clinical guarantees, but they point in a consistent direction across multiple well-designed studies.

The practical challenge is invisibility. Low vitamin D status produces no clear signal until decline is already established. This is why testing matters more than dosing by assumption. Most adults who are deficient will move meaningfully with somewhere around 4,000 IU per day, but because of substantial genetic variation in how efficiently people convert the precursor, some will need considerably more. The only reliable way to know where you stand, and whether a dose is actually working, is to measure blood levels before starting and again roughly a month later. A broadly useful target is above 75 nmol/L, though what counts as optimal is still debated.

Vitamin D is not a supplement that solves problems you can feel. It is one of the underlying regulators of systems that matter for long-term function: immune competence, bone remodelling, cardiovascular health, cognitive reserve. Getting it right is a quiet investment in your future biology, not a quick fix for today.
