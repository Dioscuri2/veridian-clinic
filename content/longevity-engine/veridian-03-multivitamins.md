## 1. BLOG POST

**Title:** "Multivitamins are useless" is officially outdated

The idea that multivitamins are a waste of money has been a dominant view in medicine for the better part of a decade. It was partly justified: early studies showed no benefit in people who were already well-nourished, and the supplement industry had encouraged overclaiming for years. But three large, well-designed randomised controlled trials, collectively known as the COSMOS studies, in adults over 65, have updated that picture in a way that is difficult to dismiss.

The trials ran for approximately two years each, using standard off-the-shelf multivitamins against placebo. What they found was a meaningful slowing of cognitive ageing. Global cognitive ageing slowed by roughly two years in the supplementing group compared to placebo. Episodic memory ageing, the recall of experiences, conversations, and people, which is precisely the type of memory that slips first with age, slowed by roughly five years. A follow-up analysis also found a modest slowing of epigenetic ageing. These are not the kind of effects that medicine typically attributes to a basic, inexpensive supplement.

The likely explanation is not that multivitamins are a form of magic. It is that widespread micronutrient shortfalls are more common than assumed, and that slow deficiency produces slow, invisible damage. Around 50% of people do not get enough magnesium. Around 35% fall short on vitamin K. Around 60% are below optimal for vitamin E. Around 25% do not meet recommended intake for vitamin C. These nutrients run background machinery: nerve signalling, immune competence, DNA repair, cellular energy production. Running them below optimal for years produces the kind of quiet, incremental deterioration that shows up in cognitive function and biological ageing data before it shows up anywhere obvious.

This does not make a multivitamin a substitute for a good diet. It does not outperform sleep, movement, or blood glucose management. And it does not address clinically significant deficiencies of specific nutrients, which require targeted supplementation at measured doses. What it does is function as low-effort, low-cost insurance against nutritional gaps that most people cannot reliably identify through diet alone, in a food environment shaped by processing and soil depletion.

The more useful framing is not whether you should take a multivitamin. It is whether you know which nutrients you are actually short on. Testing tells you that. A targeted approach based on your actual levels will outperform a blanket supplement, but a blanket supplement is considerably better than assuming your diet covers everything while running undetected gaps for years.
